## **Introduction to Ethernet Standards**

Ethernet is the most popular wired networking standard in the world, used to connect almost every type of device. Although there are many variants that differ in speed, cabling type (copper or fiber optic), and connectors, universal compatibility is guaranteed by the existence of a single set of standards.

- **The Standardization Body:** Ethernet standards are designed and documented by the **IEEE 802.3** committee (Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers). It is thanks to this body that devices from different manufacturers can communicate with each other on the same network.

---

## **Common Examples of Ethernet Standards**

There are several standards depending on the transmission medium and the required speed:

- **1000Base‑T:** A Gigabit Ethernet network that operates over twisted‑pair copper cables at a speed of 1 Gbps.
- **10GBASE‑T:** A more modern version that also uses twisted‑pair copper cables but achieves a speed of 10 Gbps.
- **1000Base‑SX:** A Gigabit Ethernet standard (1 Gbps) that uses fiber optic instead of copper.

---

## **Decoding Standard Names**

Although the names do not always reflect every technical detail, it is possible to analyze the IEEE nomenclature to understand the main characteristics of the standard.

## **1. Speed**

The initial number usually indicates the transmission speed of the network:

- **1000** corresponds to 1000 Mbps, i.e., **1 Gbps**.
- **10G** indicates a speed of **10 Gbps**.

## **2. Transmission (Base)**

The term **"Base"** in the name refers to **baseband**. This means that a single frequency is used to send data over the cable. This approach is in contrast to _Broadband_, which instead uses multiple frequencies for communication.

## **3. Transmission Medium (Suffixes)**

The final letters often indicate the type of cable or optical technology used:

- **T:** Indicates the use of **twisted‑pair copper** cable.
- **F:** Often indicates the use of **fiber optic**.
- **SX:** Refers to the use of **short‑wavelength** light over a fiber optic connection.

---

## **Note on Scientific Precision**

The IEEE attempts to make names recognizable, but this is not a formal requirement and the names do not always follow a perfect logic. To know the specific and complete technical details of a standard, it is always necessary to consult the full documentation produced by the IEEE and not rely solely on interpretation of the name.
