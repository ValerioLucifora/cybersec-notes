## 1. Fundamentals and International Standards

Wireless networks are governed by international standards managed by the **IEEE** (Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers), specifically within the **802.11** committee. Whenever 802.11 standards are mentioned, they refer to technologies associated with wireless networks (Wi‑Fi).

In addition to the IEEE, there is an organization called the **Wi‑Fi Alliance**, which is responsible for testing devices to ensure their **interoperability**. Devices that pass these tests carry the official Wi‑Fi certification mark.

## 2. Evolution of Wi‑Fi Standards

Recently, to make it easier for users to understand, generational names have been introduced alongside the technical standard designations:

- **Obsolete standards:** The **802.11a, 802.11b, and 802.11g** standards are rarely used today and are colloquially referred to as Wi‑Fi 1, 2, and 3.
- **Modern standards (Wi‑Fi 4 – Wi‑Fi 7):** These include the standards from **802.11n through 802.11be**.
- **Frequencies and performance:** The various standards primarily operate on the **2.4 GHz, 5 GHz, and 6 GHz** frequency bands. Differences in link rates depend on the number of antennas and radios used; with the introduction of new standards, theoretical speeds have steadily increased.

## 3. Mobile Networks: 4G and 5G

In addition to local Wi‑Fi, wireless connectivity is provided by mobile network operators through cellular technologies:

- **4G LTE (Long‑Term Evolution):** This represented the convergence of GSM and CDMA standards into a single global standard. It supports download speeds of approximately **150 Mbps**, while **LTE Advanced** can double that speed to **300 Mbps**.
- **5G:** Introduced in 2020, it has dramatically improved performance, with a theoretical target of reaching **10 Gbps**. In common deployments, speeds range between **100 and 900 Mbps**.
- **Impact on IoT:** Thanks to the high bandwidth of 5G, the limitations for the **Internet of Things (IoT)** have been greatly reduced, allowing more data to be transferred, notifications to be received faster, and larger amounts of information to be processed in the cloud.

## 4. Satellite Connectivity

For remote sites lacking traditional terrestrial network access, satellite networking is used.

- **Characteristics:** It requires the installation of a satellite dish. Although it offers reasonable speeds (e.g., 100 Mbps downlink and 5 Mbps uplink), it is more expensive and complex than terrestrial networks.
- **Latency:** One of the main drawbacks is the delay in data transmission. Traditional technologies have a latency of approximately **500 ms total** (250 ms for transmission and 250 ms for reception). More modern technologies, such as **Starlink**, offer much lower latency, between **20 and 40 milliseconds**.
- **Line of Sight and Rain Fade:** Satellite communication requires a direct **line of sight** to the satellite. Any physical obstruction or severe weather event (such as a thunderstorm) can cause what is known as **"rain fade"** – a temporary loss of connectivity until the disturbance has passed.
